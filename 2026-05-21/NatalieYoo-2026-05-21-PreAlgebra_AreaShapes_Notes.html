Area of Shapes — Triangle & Trapezoid (with TWO Interpretations)

Standard: CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.6.G.A.1 — Find the area of triangles, special quadrilaterals, and polygons by composing into rectangles or decomposing into triangles.
1. What IS Area?
★ The big idea
Area = the number of unit squares that fit inside a shape. If the unit is meters, the squares are 1 m × 1 m, and we measure area in m². If the unit is centimeters, the squares are 1 cm × 1 cm and we measure in cm².
A 4 m × 2 m rectangle holds 8 unit squares
Count them. The rectangle has 4 columns and 2 rows of 1 m × 1 m squares — that's 8 squares. Each square = 1 m² of area, so total area = 8 m².

The same idea works for ANY shape. We just need a way to count the squares — even when the shape doesn't fit nicely on a grid (triangle, trapezoid, etc.). That's where the formulas come from.
For a rectangle: $\text{Area} = b \times h$
2. Triangle Area — Any Base Works!
★ Key insight #1 — Triangle = half of a parallelogram
Take any triangle. Make a copy, rotate it 180° and place it next to the original. The two triangles form a parallelogram (or rectangle) with the same base and height. So:
Parallelogram area = $b \times h$.
One triangle = half of that.
$\text{Area}_\triangle = \dfrac{b \times h}{2}$
★ Key insight #2 — ANY of the 3 sides can be the base
A triangle has three sides. ANY one of them can be called the base — but then the height MUST be the perpendicular distance from that base to the opposite vertex. The same triangle gives you three different (b, h) pairs, but they all multiply to the same area.
Bottom side as base
Left slant side as base
Right slant side as base
$b_1 \cdot h_1 = b_2 \cdot h_2 = b_3 \cdot h_3$ — three different multiplications, same area. Pick whichever pair is easiest to read off the figure.
Worked Example — Triangle b = 10 cm, h = 4 cm
Area = $\dfrac{10 \times 4}{2} = \dfrac{40}{2} = $ 20 cm²
Important: 4 cm is the perpendicular distance from the chosen 10-cm base to the top vertex — NOT the slanted side.
3. Trapezoid Area — TWO Ways to See It
★ The formula (memorize the IDEA, not the symbols)
A trapezoid has two parallel sides — call them $b_1$ (top) and $b_2$ (bottom) — and a perpendicular height $h$ between them.
$\text{Area}_\text{trap} = \dfrac{(b_1 + b_2) \times h}{2}$
WHY does it look like that? There are two equally good explanations. Pick the one that "clicks" for you — both prove the formula is correct.
WAY A — Half of TWO trapezoids (flip into a parallelogram)
Take the trapezoid. Make an identical copy. Flip the copy upside down and slide it next to the original. The two trapezoids together form a parallelogram.
  • Parallelogram area = base × height = $(b_1 + b_2) \cdot h$
  • One trapezoid = HALF of that → divide by 2
  • Area = $\dfrac{(b_1 + b_2) \cdot h}{2}$ ✓
WAY B — SUM of two triangles (cut along the diagonal)
Take the trapezoid. Draw a diagonal from one corner to the opposite corner. This cuts the trapezoid into two triangles sharing the same perpendicular height $h$ — but with different bases ($b_1$ and $b_2$).
  • Top triangle area = $\dfrac{b_1 \cdot h}{2}$
  • Bottom triangle area = $\dfrac{b_2 \cdot h}{2}$
  • Trapezoid = sum: $\dfrac{b_1 \cdot h}{2} + \dfrac{b_2 \cdot h}{2} = \dfrac{(b_1 + b_2) \cdot h}{2}$ ✓
Same answer. Different picture in your head. Both interpretations live behind that one formula.
Worked Example — Trapezoid b₁ = 3, b₂ = 6, h = 4 (all in cm)
Area = $\dfrac{(3 + 6) \times 4}{2} = \dfrac{9 \times 4}{2} = \dfrac{36}{2} = $ 18 cm²
Way A check: two trapezoids → 9 × 4 = 36 parallelogram, half = 18. ✓
Way B check: triangle 3·4/2 = 6 + triangle 6·4/2 = 12, sum = 18. ✓
★ One-Page Summary
  • Area = count the unit squares (m², cm², in², …).
  • Rectangle: $\text{Area} = b \cdot h$
  • Triangle: $\text{Area} = \dfrac{b \cdot h}{2}$   (any side can be base; height must be perpendicular)
  • Trapezoid: $\text{Area} = \dfrac{(b_1 + b_2) \cdot h}{2}$   (Way A: half of doubled-up parallelogram · Way B: sum of two triangles)
